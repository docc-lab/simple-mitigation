\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

\subsection{Symptom and source}
\label{sec:motiv-problem}

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{l|cc}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Steady} & \textbf{Tail} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Intrinsic} & Replication & Harvesting CPUs or CPU shares \\
    \textbf{Extrinsic} & Migration & Migration, Harvesting CPUs or CPU shares \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Mapping of a contention's source (rows) and symptom (columns) to the mitigation it requires.}
  \label{tab:grid}
\end{table}

CPU contention on a latency-critical service has two independent dimensions
that together decide which mitigation can help (Table~\ref{tab:grid}).
Each cell needs its own mitigation because the cells differ in what is
starved and for how long: a steady intrinsic symptom lacks sustained
capacity, which only \emph{replication} adds, whereas a tail extrinsic
symptom needs isolated CPU capacity for milliseconds, so \emph{harvesting}
confines the neighbour for the burst and returns the victim's idle cores
afterwards. A mismatched mitigation does not help, or is much less
efficient than the matched one. Replication, which reacts in seconds,
cannot act in time against a tail contention and leaves the contended
instance serving; harvesting against a steady intrinsic contention
saturates at the node's already overwhelmed cores without giving the
service the capacity headroom it needs.

We validate this grid on its two diagonal cells, tail extrinsic and
steady intrinsic, with both actuators, so that each meets one cell that
matches it and one that does not, by answering two evaluation questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{EQ1.} Under each contention scenario (symptom $\times$
  source) and for the same SLO target, how much of the degradation do the
  matched mitigation and a mismatched one remove, respectively?
  \item \textbf{EQ2.} To reach the same SLO target, how large a core budget
  do the matched mitigation and a mismatched one require, respectively?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:motiv-setup}

Five CloudLab c6320 nodes (2$\times$14-core Xeon E5-2683\,v3, SMT on,
2.0\,GHz pinned, Linux~6.12 with the \hc module, Kubernetes~1.25). The
victim is DeathStarBench \textsf{hotelReservation}'s \textsf{search}, with
an in-pod scorer that reports per-request processing time as an
interference score $e_{\mathrm{iso}}$; replicas go to a reserved node.
\emph{Tail extrinsic:} the victim runs two threads at 2000\,rps (0.96~cores,
knee ${\approx}6000$); seven \textsf{socialNetwork} and \textsf{sockshop}
services share the node, free over a 12-core pool that includes the
victim's sibling threads, and burst for 6\,s every 60\,s (6.7--7.6~cores,
10\% of the time): two bursts with mitigation disabled, then two with it
enabled. \emph{Steady intrinsic:} a one-thread victim (knee 4000\,rps)
receives 800\,rps for 20\,s, then 6000\,rps for 120\,s, next to a
lower-priority busy-loop pod on four threads; mitigation is enabled from
the start. \emph{Harvesting} makes the victim the \hc primary and the
co-tenants secondaries, which \hc confines to the pool minus the victim's
threads and siblings (tail cell), or lets the victim take one of the
busy-loop pod's threads whenever it is runnable (steady cell).
\emph{Replication} scales a copy on the replica node: reactively on
$e_{\mathrm{iso}}>0.15$, up to three replicas (tail cell), or one replica
pre-provisioned (steady cell). Every arm has the same victim core budget
and co-tenant placement. We report service-level latency relative to the
run's quiet phase: p95 inside the bursts (SLO 1.3$\times$) and p50 of the
overload (SLO 2.5$\times$).

\subsection{Tail symptom, extrinsic source}
\label{sec:motiv-tail}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/motiv3_bars.pdf}
  \caption{Tail extrinsic: victim latency in and between the bursts relative to its quiet phase, and cores per arm. Dashed: SLO 1.3$\times$.}
  \label{fig:motiv-bars}
\end{figure}

Unmitigated, the bursts raise the victim's p95 by 1.4--1.6$\times$ while p50
moves 1.1$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:motiv-bars}): a tail symptom from
per-request slowdown, although the victim uses under half its cores.
Harvesting, the matched mitigation, holds p95 at 1.07--1.12$\times$ with no
extra cores; the co-tenants leave the victim's cores and siblings while it
is busy, still receive its 0.4 idle cores, and keep 5.9--6.1 of their
6.7--7.6 cores. Replication, the mismatched one, cannot help the burst that
triggers it: the first replica serves 3--5\,s into a 6\,s burst, the
contended instance keeps serving its share, and p95 stays at
1.3--1.4$\times$; three pre-provisioned replicas on an earlier cluster
still gave 1.30$\times$ (none 1.84$\times$). It also raises the latency
between bursts to 1.10$\times$, as scale-in lags by 30\,s.

\subsection{Steady symptom, intrinsic source}
\label{sec:motiv-steady}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/motiv3_int.pdf}
  \caption{Steady intrinsic: p50 of the overload relative to the quiet phase (dashed: SLO 2.5$\times$), and latency and cores over time.}
  \label{fig:motiv-int}
\end{figure}

Unmitigated, the victim is past its knee and the whole distribution shifts:
p50 is 8--9$\times$ quiet in two trials and 95$\times$ in the third
(Fig.~\ref{fig:motiv-int}). Replication, the matched mitigation, brings it
to 2.4$\times$ (5.4\,ms) and meets the SLO; harvesting, the mismatched one,
to 3.1$\times$ (7.0\,ms) and misses it. The replica is a second full
instance on its own thread (0.88 + 0.88 cores), whereas the harvested
thread is shared and reclaimed on demand and the victim reaches 1.37 cores
of it. A sweep from 3000 to 8000\,rps shows the two tie up to 3500\,rps,
where one harvested thread suffices, and the replication-to-harvesting p50
ratio falls to 0.77 at 6000 and 0.63 at 8000. Harvesting costs the
busy-loop pod a thread only while the victim needs it; replication costs
0.9~cores on a second node for the whole overload.

\paragraph{Takeaway.}
The diagonal of Table~\ref{tab:grid} holds: harvesting removes a tail
extrinsic symptom at its source with no extra cores where replication
recovers at most a third of it, and replication removes a steady intrinsic
symptom where harvesting saturates at the node's idle cores. The symptom
decides which statistic is hurt and whether a reactive mechanism can act
in time; the source decides whether confinement or capacity is the remedy.
A mitigation controller therefore needs more than one actuator, and a
signal that places the contention in the grid before it acts.
